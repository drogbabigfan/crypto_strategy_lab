Deep Reinforcement Learning Architectures for High-Frequency Cryptocurrency Trading: A Comprehensive Analysis of the Mahayana et al. Framework and Technical Indicator State Spaces1. Introduction1.1 The Evolution of Algorithmic Trading in Digital Asset MarketsThe advent of cryptocurrency has fundamentally altered the landscape of financial speculation and market microstructure analysis. Unlike traditional equity or foreign exchange markets, which are bound by session times, regulatory circuit breakers, and centralized clearing houses, the cryptocurrency market operates as a continuous, decentralized, and highly fragmented global ecosystem. This 24/7 trading environment, characterized by extreme volatility and inefficiencies, has become a fertile testing ground for advanced algorithmic trading strategies.Historically, quantitative finance relied on econometric models such as the Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) or Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (GARCH) to forecast time-series data. While effective in capturing linear dependencies and volatility clustering in mature markets, these stochastic models often fail to account for the chaotic, non-linear, and regime-switching nature of crypto-asset price formations. As the market matured, the focus shifted toward Machine Learning (ML) approaches, including Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and Random Forests, which could model complex, high-dimensional feature spaces.However, the most significant paradigm shift has been the transition from predictive modeling to control modeling. Prediction asks, "What will the price be at time $t+1$?" Control asks, "Given the current state, what action maximizes future reward?" This distinction is the domain of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL).1.2 The Mahayana et al. Theoretical Framework: Deep Reinforcement Learning in FinanceTo understand the specific design choices made by Mahayana et al., one must first establish a robust theoretical understanding of the underlying algorithms. DRL in finance is not merely applying a standard library; it requires careful adaptation of the Markov Decision Process (MDP) to financial time series.2.1 The Financial Markov Decision Process (MDP)The core of any RL problem is the tuple $(S, A, P, R, \gamma)$, representing the State, Action, Transition Probability, Reward, and Discount Factor.State Space ($S$): In the context of Mahayana et al., $S_t$ is not just the price $P_t$. It is a high-dimensional vector containing the 18 technical indicators, and potentially the agent's current portfolio status (cash vs. coin holdings). This vector, $s_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$, must satisfy the Markov property: the future state depends only on the current state and action, not the history. Technical indicators are essentially "memory encoders," compressing historical price action into the current state vector to satisfy this property.Action Space ($A$): Trading actions can be discrete or continuous.Discrete: $\mathcal{A} = \{0: \text{Hold}, 1: \text{Buy}, 2: \text{Sell}\}$. This is typical for DQN.Continuous: $\mathcal{A} \in [-1, 1]$, representing the fraction of portfolio to invest. PPO, used by Mahayana, is particularly adept at handling continuous action spaces, though it can also handle discrete ones via Softmax output layers.Reward Function ($R$): The most critical component. $R(s_t, a_t)$ defines the goal. In finance, this is usually realized PnL (Profit and Loss) or Sharpe Ratio. Mahayana et al. introduce specific innovations here (discussed in Section 3).2.2 Policy Gradient Methods vs. Value-Based MethodsThe literature on crypto-trading RL is divided between two main families:Value-Based (e.g., DQN, Double DQN): These methods learn a Q-function $Q(s, a)$ that estimates the expected future reward of taking action $a$ in state $s$. The policy is deterministic: $\pi(s) = \text{argmax}_a Q(s, a)$. Satarov et al. 5 utilized this approach. The limitation is that Q-learning struggles with stochastic environments where the "best" action is probabilistic.Policy-Based (e.g., REINFORCE): These methods learn the policy function $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ directly, optimizing the weights $\theta$ of a neural network to maximize expected return $J(\theta)$. This allows for stochastic policies, which are essential in poker or trading, where "bluffing" or mixed strategies can be optimal.2.3 Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)Mahayana et al. selected PPO 1, a state-of-the-art policy gradient method developed by OpenAI. PPO is designed to solve the problem of "destructive updates" in standard policy gradient methods.In financial time series, data is extremely noisy (low Signal-to-Noise Ratio). A standard policy gradient update might observe a massive profit from a lucky trade and take a large gradient step, essentially "overwriting" the agent's cautious policy with a reckless one. If the market regime shifts the next minute, the agent fails catastrophically.PPO mitigates this using a Clipped Surrogate Objective:$$L^{CLIP}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \left[ \min(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t, \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t) \right]$$$r_t(\theta)$: The ratio of the probability of the action under the new policy vs. the old policy.$\hat{A}_t$: The Advantage function, estimating how much better the chosen action was compared to the average baseline.Clipping ($1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon$): This forces the new policy to stay "proximal" (close) to the old policy. The agent is not allowed to change its behavior too drastically based on a single batch of data.relevance to Mahayana's Results: This stability is crucial for 1-minute crypto data, where volatility spikes (flash crashes) can produce misleading gradients. PPO theoretically allows the Mahayana agent to learn a robust trading strategy that ignores transient noise.3. The Mahayana et al. Framework: Methodology and ArchitectureThe Mahayana et al. study 1 implements a specific instance of the PPO architecture tailored for high-frequency Bitcoin trading. We break down the core components of their methodology.3.1 Data Granularity and PreprocessingThe researchers utilized 1-minute candlestick data for the BTC/USDT pair.Implication: 1-minute data represents the "microstructure" view of the market. At this level, price movements are heavily influenced by order book dynamics, bid-ask spreads, and algorithmic latency, rather than fundamental valuation.State Representation: The input to the neural network was not just the raw OHLC (Open, High, Low, Close) data, but a derived feature vector of 18 technical indicators.13.2 Reward Engineering: The Penalty for "Holding Losers"One of the most distinct aspects of the Mahayana study, as highlighted in snippets 1 and 7, is the design of the reward function. Standard DRL agents often succumb to "laziness"—learning that the safest way to avoid negative rewards (losses) is to never trade (holding cash). Alternatively, they might hold a losing position indefinitely ("bag holding") hoping for a mean-reversion that never comes.To combat this, Mahayana et al. introduced a composite reward function:PnL Reward: Proportional to the profit or loss of the position.Time-Decay Penalty: A negative reward term that increases for every time step $t$ that the agent maintains a position with Unrealized PnL $< 0$.The Mathematical Logic:Let $R_t$ be the reward at time $t$.$$R_t = \Delta \text{PortfolioValue} - \lambda \cdot \mathbb{I}(\text{Loss}_t) \cdot (t - t_{\text{entry}})$$Where $\lambda$ is a penalty coefficient and $\mathbb{I}(\text{Loss}_t)$ is an indicator function that is 1 if the position is losing and 0 otherwise.Behavioral Consequence: This forces the PPO agent to become "impatient" with losses. It mimics the human trader discipline of "cutting losses early." However, in a noisy 1-minute environment, this can lead to "whipsaw" losses—selling at the bottom of a random dip just before the price recovers.3.3 Experimental Results: The Failure to Generate AlphaDespite the sophistication of PPO and the 18-indicator state space, Mahayana et al. reported that the model "failed to outperform the Buy and Hold strategy".2 The PPO agent could not consistently generate profit in excess of simply holding Bitcoin, especially when transaction costs were factored in.This negative result is a critical data point. It suggests that:Market Efficiency: The 1-minute Bitcoin market may be efficient enough that simple technical indicators (the "Standard 18") do not contain sufficient predictive alpha to overcome trading fees.Reward Misalignment: The penalty for holding losers might have been too aggressive, forcing the agent to realize losses that would have otherwise recovered.Benchmark Difficulty: During bull runs, "Buy and Hold" is an exceptionally high bar for any active trading strategy to clear.4. Comparative Analysis of DRL Architectures in Crypto TradingTo validate the findings of Mahayana et al., it is necessary to compare their results with other researchers utilizing similar state spaces but different RL architectures.5.1 Mahayana (PPO) vs. Satarov (DQN)Satarov et al. 5 utilized Deep Q-Networks (DQN).Architecture: DQN works by learning the Value of actions. It is "off-policy," meaning it can learn from a replay buffer of old experiences.Reward Structure: Satarov penalized "consecutive actions" (e.g., Buy-Buy-Buy) to prevent churning. Mahayana penalized "holding time."Outcome: Satarov reported beating traditional technical strategies, whereas Mahayana failed to beat Buy-and-Hold.Insight: The discrete action space of DQN (Buy/Sell/Hold) might be better suited for the binary nature of trend decisions than the continuous probability distributions of PPO in this specific noise-heavy environment.5.2 The Alonso-Monsalve Connection (CNN-LSTM)Alonso-Monsalve et al. 3 utilized the exact same 18-indicator set but applied a supervised learning approach (Classification) rather than Reinforcement Learning.Method: They used CNNs to extract spatial features from the indicator matrix and LSTMs to capture temporal dependencies.Result: They achieved high accuracy in predicting trend direction (Up/Down).Implication for Mahayana: The fact that Alonso-Monsalve achieved success with these 18 indicators suggests that the state space contains valid signal. The failure of Mahayana's agent is likely due to the difficulty of the Control problem (RL) vs. the Prediction problem (Supervised Learning). In RL, the agent must not only predict the direction but also manage risk, timing, and accumulated reward over a trajectory.6. Challenges and Critical LimitationsThe "Standard 18" indicator set, while comprehensive, suffers from inherent limitations when applied to 1-minute cryptocurrency trading via DRL.6.1 Lag and The Moving Average Problem10 of the 18 indicators are Moving Averages. MAs are inherently lagging indicators. They mathematically cannot signal a turn until after the turn has occurred.In a 1-minute timeframe, a significant price move (e.g., a 0.5% spike) might happen in a single candle. The SMA-5 will not reflect this fully for 5 minutes.By the time the PPO agent receives the "Buy" signal from the lagged indicators, the micro-trend may already be exhausted. This explains the underperformance relative to Buy-and-Hold.6.2 The Transaction Cost BarrierSnippet 5 notes that many studies assume "commission free markets" or low fees (0.15%). In reality, taking liquidity (Market Orders) on crypto exchanges often incurs fees of 0.04% to 0.1%.The "Standard 18" indicators generate frequent signals. If the agent trades every time the Stoch-K crosses Stoch-D on a 1-minute chart, it might trade 50 times a day.$50 \text{ trades} \times 0.1\% \text{ fee} = 5\% \text{ loss}$ per day due to fees alone. The indicators must generate enormous alpha to overcome this friction.6.3 Non-Stationarity and OverfittingCrypto markets undergo "Regime Shifts." A strategy trained on 2021 data (Bull Market) will fail in 2022 (Bear Market) because the statistical properties of the indicators change.Example: In a strong uptrend, RSI > 70 is not a sell signal; it is a sign of strength. In a ranging market, RSI > 70 is a sell signal.The Mahayana agent, trained on a specific slice of history, likely overfit the "meaning" of these 18 indicators for that specific period. When the market regime changed in the test set, the policy collapsed.7. Future Directions: Beyond the Standard 18The analysis of Mahayana et al. suggests that while DRL is a powerful framework, the "Standard 18" technical indicators are insufficient for 1-minute alpha generation in efficient markets. Future research is pivoting toward:Order Book Dynamics: Instead of lagging indicators, agents should use Level 2 (Order Book) data—bid/ask depth and order flow imbalance. This data is leading, not lagging.Sentiment Integration: Incorporating real-time social sentiment (Twitter/X, Reddit) to gauge market psychology.11Transformer Architectures: Replacing LSTMs with Transformers (Attention mechanisms) allows the agent to dynamically "attend" to different indicators. For example, the agent might learn to ignore RSI when Volume is effectively zero, or focus entirely on Momentum during a volatility spike.8. ConclusionThe Mahayana et al. study serves as a vital benchmark in the application of Deep Reinforcement Learning to cryptocurrency trading. By coupling the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm with a high-dimensional state space of 18 technical indicators, the researchers attempted to automate the extraction of alpha from 1-minute Bitcoin price action.This report has identified the specific composition of that state space—comprising a dense cluster of Moving Averages (SMA/WMA 5-60), Momentum Oscillators (RSI, Stoch, Williams %R), and Trend metrics (MACD, CCI). While these indicators provide a theoretically robust view of market kinematics, the empirical results—specifically the failure to outperform Buy-and-Hold strategies—reveal the limitations of lagging technical analysis in high-frequency domains.The Mahayana framework demonstrates that "better math" (PPO vs DQN) does not necessarily yield "better profits" if the input features (lagging indicators) and environmental constraints (transaction costs, noise) are not fundamentally addressed.
